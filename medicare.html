HEALTHCARE ANALYTICS CASE STUDY

Medicare Provider Payment Analytics

Why do some Medicare providers receive substantially more in Medicare payments than others? This project explored whether provider volume, specialty, and payment intensity could help explain the differences.

Python Pandas NumPy Matplotlib Statistics Healthcare Analytics
View GitHub Repository Read Published Analysis

PROJECT OVERVIEW

The Question

Medicare payment totals vary considerably across providers. At first glance, a simple explanation would be that providers who see more beneficiaries or perform more services receive more Medicare payments.

I wanted to determine whether volume was truly the entire story. I examined whether total Medicare payments were associated with provider service volume, beneficiary volume, provider specialty, and the amount of Medicare payment associated with each service.

Analytical goal Distinguish payment differences associated with delivering more care from differences associated with specialty and payment intensity.

DATASET

The Analytical Sample

5,000 Medicare providers analyzed
87 Provider specialties represented
153 Median beneficiaries per provider
451 Median services per provider

FINDING 01

Medicare Payments Were Highly Skewed

One of the first important signals appeared when I compared the average Medicare payment with the median.

$111,655.73 Average Medicare payment
$30,645.92 Median Medicare payment
$997,573.34 99th percentile payment

The average was approximately 3.6 times the median, indicating that unusually large payment values were pulling the mean upward. The distribution was strongly right-skewed, so the median provided a better description of the typical provider than the average alone.

Approximately 99% of providers had total Medicare payments at or below $997,573.34. Only 50 providers in the 5,000-provider sample were above that threshold.

Why I used logarithmic visualization Payment values ranged from relatively small amounts to more than $123 million. A traditional linear histogram compressed most providers into one area. Logarithmically spaced bins allowed the underlying distribution to remain visible without deleting the extreme observations.

FINDING 02

Service Volume Had the Strongest Association With Payment

0.256 Pearson: services vs. payments
0.887 Spearman: services vs. payments
0.813 Spearman: beneficiaries vs. payments
0.751 Spearman: beneficiaries vs. services

Pearson correlation suggested only a weak positive linear relationship between total services and total Medicare payments.

However, both variables were highly skewed and the relationship was not well represented by one straight line. Spearman correlation, which compares ranked values, showed a very strong positive association of 0.887.

Interpretation Providers that ranked higher in service volume generally also ranked higher in total Medicare payments. This was an association, not evidence that service volume alone caused higher payments.

FINDING 03

Provider Specialty Also Mattered

I grouped providers by specialty and compared median Medicare payments. Before interpreting the rankings, I checked how many providers represented each specialty.

Several initially high-ranking categories contained only one, two, or three providers. To avoid allowing extremely small groups to dominate the comparison, I required at least 10 providers per specialty for the final descriptive ranking.

Finding Result
Highest median total payment Ambulatory Surgical Centers — $764,227.66
Highest median payment per service Ambulatory Surgical Centers — $658.70
Second-highest median payment per service Neurosurgery — approximately $227.68
Selected procedure-heavy specialties Median total payment — $74,582.77
Selected primary-care specialties Median total payment — $35,325.46

The procedure-heavy comparison was descriptive. It showed a difference in this sample, but it did not establish that performing procedures caused higher Medicare reimbursement.

FINDING 04

Volume Was Not the Entire Story

Total payment can rise simply because a provider delivers more services. To separate volume from payment intensity, I created normalized measures including Medicare payment per beneficiary and Medicare payment per service.

If volume explained everything, payment per service would be relatively similar across specialties. Instead, the analysis showed substantial specialty-level differences.

Two components emerged

Higher Medicare payment totals appeared to reflect both how much care a provider delivered and the payment intensity associated with the type of care being delivered.

FINDING 05

What Made the Highest-Payment Providers Different?

I compared the typical provider in the full sample with the 50 providers above the 99th-percentile payment threshold.

Measure Full Sample Median Upper 1% Median
Beneficiaries 153 740.5
Services 451 19,846
Total Medicare Payment $30,645.92 Approximately $1.73 million
Payment per Beneficiary Approximately $188.68 Approximately $2,625
Payment per Service Approximately $66.48 Approximately $100.67

The upper 1% differed from the typical provider in both volume and payment intensity. This reinforced the idea that extremely high Medicare payment totals did not have one universal explanation.

ANALYTICAL DECISION

Investigating Outliers Instead of Automatically Removing Them

The largest total Medicare payment in the sample was approximately $123 million. Because the observation was extreme, I investigated the record rather than immediately deleting it.

I reviewed the related beneficiary, service, payment-per- beneficiary, and payment-per-service values. I did not have evidence that the observation was a data-entry error, so I retained it.

Analytical principle An outlier is a reason to investigate a record, not automatically a reason to remove it. Robust statistics and logarithmic visualizations allowed the extreme observation to remain in the analysis without hiding the broader pattern.

CONCLUSION

Volume Explains a Lot — But Not Everything

The strongest finding from the analysis was that provider volume, particularly service volume, was strongly associated with total Medicare payments.

However, specialty-level differences and variation in payment per service showed that volume alone could not explain every payment difference.

The descriptive evidence suggested that Medicare payment patterns reflect a combination of how much care providers deliver and the type or payment intensity of that care.

Main takeaway

Finding a statistical pattern is only the beginning. Additional information on procedure mix, patient complexity, geography, reimbursement policy, and case mix would be needed to better understand why those payment differences exist.
