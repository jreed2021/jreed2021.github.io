01 / THE QUESTION

What is driving Medicare Part D spending?

For a simulated Medicare prescription drug benefit analytics team, the central business question was whether changes in overall expenditures were more closely associated with the number of prescription fills, average spending per fill, or both. The analysis also asked which medications contribute the largest spending amounts and where additional review may be useful.

Business objectives

  • Measure annual spending and prescription utilization trends.
  • Distinguish high-volume drugs from high-spending drugs.
  • Identify major year-over-year drug spending increases.
  • Build an executive-facing Tableau dashboard.

Analytical controls

  • Prevent double-counting from mixed aggregation levels.
  • Reconcile CMS-reported spending per claim.
  • Retain missing historical observations without fabricating zeros.
  • Interpret CMS outlier flags cautiously.

02 / DATA & METHODS

From raw federal data to reproducible reporting

Source: CMS Medicare Part D Spending by Drug, published with a data dictionary and methodology document. The original dataset contained 14,536 rows and 46 columns, with annual measures spanning 2020–2024.

Profile and validate in Python. Examined data types, missing values, duplicates, manufacturer rows, and average spending per claim.

Preserve the raw data in MySQL. Loaded 14,536 source records before transforming into 72,680 drug-year records.

Reproduce financial analysis in SQL. Used CTEs, conditional aggregation and window functions to calculate trends, rank drugs, and compare spending and fills.

Communicate through Tableau. Built an executive dashboard with annual spending, KPI cards, drug rankings and a spending-driver comparison.

Critical quality finding: Adding overall drug rows to their manufacturer-level breakdowns approximately doubled the 2024 spending total ($577.34B vs. $288.67B). Analysis at the program-wide level therefore used Mftr_Name = 'Overall'. These are overlapping aggregation levels, not duplicate individual claims.

In 2024, prescription fills increased 5.87% while average gross spending per fill decreased approximately 1.14%. Higher aggregate utilization more than offset the decline in average spending per fill. This arithmetic relationship does not establish the reasons for changes in drug pricing or prescribing.

Spending is concentrated among a small number of drugs

The ten highest-spending drug records represented 28.51% of 2024 reported gross spending. Eliquis ranked first, at approximately $20.77B. For context, atorvastatin had far more prescription fills but much lower average spending per fill.

04 / VALIDATION

Financial accuracy and limitations

Reconciliation

All 3,625 overall drug records passed the 2024 average-spending-per-claim reconciliation at a $0.01 tolerance. The maximum absolute difference was approximately $0.00000412.

CMS outlier indicators

Among overall drug records, 395 were flagged in 2024 for potentially anomalous average spending per dosage unit; another 18 had missing outlier flags. Flags are signals for cautious interpretation, not evidence of invalid claims.

Important limitations

  • Reported gross spending includes beneficiary payments and does not reflect confidential manufacturer rebates or other price concessions.
  • Historical data may be suppressed under CMS reporting rules. Missing values were not treated as confirmed zero spending.
  • Drug-level aggregates cannot establish individual beneficiary medication switching or clinical appropriateness.
  • The matched-drug growth analysis excludes products without reported figures in both years.
  • Spending per prescription fill is not identical to net price per dose or a standardized unit price.

05 / BUSINESS IMPLICATIONS

Recommendations for further investigation

Monitor financial concentration

Track high-spending drugs using both gross spending and prescription volume, and review changes in drug mix before attributing differences to prices.

Strengthen recurring controls

Separate overall and manufacturer reporting grains, preserve historical nulls, reconcile derived metrics, and document exceptions before issuing reports.

Any formulary or coverage recommendation would require additional clinical evidence, beneficiary access considerations, applicable requirements, and plan-level net spending data. This project supports prioritization for review, not automatic medication restrictions.

Takeaway: The strongest financial insight came from combining data-quality checks with utilization and spending analysis—not from ranking high-cost medications alone.
